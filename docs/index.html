Why

Room correction shouldn't need a weekend and a manual

The usual route on Linux is Room EQ Wizard: install Java, wrestle the window manager, learn its menus, measure, export, import into EasyEffects by hand, and hope you picked sensible settings. CorrectSound does the same job in one guided pass, and explains what it found.

Measures properly

Exponential sweeps, calibrated with your mic's own file, at the quietest level your room's background noise allows (often 60 dB), with signal-to-noise checked per octave. Several mic positions are averaged.

Corrects conservatively

Cuts room resonances freely, lifts broad dips only a little, never chases the dips no EQ can fill. Picks the number of filters by checking which ones hold up at other mic positions.

Verifies the result, or rolls it back

Loads the preset into EasyEffects, sets it to load automatically, then plays the sweeps again through it and compares with what it predicted. Not convinced? One key puts EasyEffects back as it was.

Guides room treatment, live

EQ can't fix everything in the bass; the room can. Live placement re-measures five times a second while you move a bass trap, a speaker or your chair, and sweeps show how long each bass note rings, so you see what each change does before you commit to it. A big number readable from across the room says how much better it got, against the pinned curve kept on screen.

How it works

From a sweep to an equalizer

Every step below is in the code, tested against a simulated room whose true response is known. Here is the whole path, from a tone gliding through the speaker to a preset in EasyEffects.

Where the sound goes

While measuring, CorrectSound plays straight to your sound card, past EasyEffects, so it hears the bare speakers. Afterwards, everything you play (Spotify, a browser, games) goes through EasyEffects with the new preset in it.

Tour

Five steps, one key each

Every screen has one main action on Enter, Esc goes back, and i explains what is going on (m for the technical version). These screenshots are the real app, running against its built-in simulated room: correctsound --simulate works without any hardware.

1

Setup

One screen with everything ticked off: the UMIK-1, its calibration, its gain, EasyEffects, and your outputs with the one EasyEffects plays to already selected. The first time, type the serial printed on your mic, and its personal calibration file is downloaded from miniDSP and saved. The mic's input volume is set to exactly 1.0 in PipeWire and ALSA, because the calibration assumes nothing amplifies the signal on the way in. It warns about apps that are playing, and pauses EasyEffects while measuring so the bare speakers are measured. Everything is remembered, so the next time setup is one Enter; everything it changes is put back when it exits, even after a crash or a closed terminal.

2

Level

Keep quiet for a few seconds while CorrectSound listens to your room. You see the background noise in the bass, the mids and the highs, live, and the level the measurement needs to stay 30 dB above it in every octave. Traffic and ventilation rumble in the bass, so that is usually what decides. A quiet room is measured at a comfortable 60 dB; a noisy one asks for up to 80, or for a quieter moment. Then press Enter: pink noise plays from one speaker and you turn the amplifier until the meter shows that level. Louder is fine, up to a comfortable 85 dB: the sweeps only gain margin. Above 95 dB it stops by itself.

3

Measure

A 5.5-second sweep plays from each speaker. Each recording is checked for clipping, for a missing sweep and for noise in every octave, and redone if needed. Moving the mic 20–30 cm and pressing a adds a position: the room sounds different a hand's width away, and averaging keeps only what is common to all of them.

4

Result

The measurement, the target and the predicted result, with the equalizer drawn below: every filter on its own (cuts in red, boosts in green) and the whole thing. How many filters and how high to correct are chosen automatically; t, h and f override them and the design updates instantly.

5

Install, verify, keep or roll back

Three steps, each with its key on the screen. a backs up your EasyEffects settings, installs the preset as CoSo-001-DDMMYYYY, loads it and makes it load automatically for that output. v plays the sweeps again, this time through EasyEffects, and compares the result with the prediction: the purple line should sit on the blue one. If you measured several mic positions, put the mic back at the first one: the comparison is made for that spot. Then keep it (Enter), or u rolls EasyEffects back to how it was: the preset it had loaded and what it loaded automatically. The new preset stays in its list, to try again later.

Beyond EQ

Below 200 Hz, the room is in charge

In the bass, sound waves are as long as the room. They bounce between the walls, pile up into resonances that boom, and cancel where a reflection arrives out of step. Two tools help with what EQ can't fix.

Live placement and room treatment p

Walk around with a bass trap and see what it does right away.

The bass at your seat is measured over and over, with one number for how uneven it is. Whatever changes the bass shows up straight away: a bass trap in a corner, a speaker 10 cm further from the wall, your chair moved forward. Pin one state with Space and compare everything else against it. Room treatment stops being guesswork and becomes a few minutes of trying.

Two ways to measure, both with sweeps, switched with m:

Fast sweeps

A one-second sweep over and over without pauses. The curve and the waterfall of how the bass decays are recomputed five times a second, so a change shows within a second, ringing included: fast enough to watch while you carry the trap around.

Sweeps

A short sweep every couple of seconds: fine detail and the waterfall, from a sweep with silence around it. Keep still while it plays, then read how much shorter each note rings than in the pinned sweep.

Whatever the mode, a big number says how many dB more even than the pinned state the bass is, large enough to read from the corner you're standing in, and the pinned curve stays on the chart behind the latest one.

Treating a room with it

  1. Put the mic on its stand at your seat. It stays there the whole time. On the measure step, press p.
  2. Let a few sweeps run with the room as it is, then press Space to pin that curve. It is what everything else is compared with.
  3. Press m for fast sweeps, and carry the bass trap from corner to corner, watching the big number and the curve against the pinned one. It follows within a second.
  4. Put the trap down where the number was highest: the decay line says which notes ring shorter than before (m again gives single sweeps, for the finest detail). Try the wall behind the speakers, behind your seat, or two traps stacked.
  5. Leave the trap where it helped most. Then run the normal measurement, so the EQ corrects what is left.

Tab switches speakers, and s pauses the sound while you carry something heavy.

The same loop works for anything that moves: speakers closer to or further from the wall, the desk, your chair. And it is honest about what doesn't help: thin foam panels barely touch the bass, and the chart shows that before you buy more of them.

Made for wide screens

On an ultrawide monitor the charts use the whole width: the bass and the waterfall side by side, with room for every peak and dip. Charts are pixel images in Ghostty and kitty, and one image can span at most 297 columns of text, so on a terminal wider than that CorrectSound sends each chart in tiles that join seamlessly. Narrow terminals work too: the big number makes way for the text below 110 columns, and terminals without images get braille charts.

Decay waterfall w

How each bass note fades after the sound stops. The back slice is the sound as it starts; each slice in front is 20 ms later. Most notes vanish within a few slices; a resonance stands out as a ridge that runs all the way to the front.

Notes that ring much longer than the rest sound boomy and smear the bass line. The list beside the waterfall names them. EQ cuts lower them; bass traps in corners, or moving things with p, treat the cause.

The waterfall shows the first 400 ms, but a room's lowest resonances can ring for well over a second. Those are measured on their own, however long they last: each one's time to fade 60 dB, next to the rest of the room's, from how its energy dies away and, as a cross-check, from how narrow its peak is. In one real living room, the side-to-side standing wave at 33 Hz rang for 1.7 s and the one running around all four walls at 45 Hz for 1.5 s, while everything else faded in 0.6 s.

Tab switches speakers on one shared scale, so a quieter speaker sits lower, and it works on saved sessions too: correctsound review, then w.

Room r

Your room from above

In the bass, a room is a handful of standing waves between its walls, each loud in some places and silent in others. Which ones you hear depends on where the speakers and your seat are. CorrectSound draws your room to scale and shows them.

Guess first, then measure

From the measurement alone, CorrectSound guesses the room's floor plan and where the speakers stand: it tries the rooms whose standing waves match the resonances it heard and, in each, the layout whose predicted bass matches what the mic heard. Which corner they are in shows in the left/right balance at the lowest standing waves: a speaker near a side wall drives the side-to-side wave harder. In one real living room it found the floor plan within 2 % and the speakers in the right corner. The guesses are listed under your own rooms; change any number with a tape measure (type it, or nudge it 5 cm at a time and watch the prediction follow) and it becomes your room, saved for the next measurement and with the session.

Which wave is which

Every resonance gets a name: "33 Hz: side to side, 5.24 m", "45 Hz: around all four walls". The plan shows where each is loud (red and blue, in opposite phase) and silent.

Where traps work

A bass trap absorbs a standing wave where it is loud. The walls are marked green where that is: the side walls for one wave, the four corners for another.

A better seat

Press v for a map of how even the bass below 80 Hz would be at every seat, and the most even one within reach. It also tries moving the whole setup along the wall.

Honest about itself

A real room is not a perfect box, so the model is compared with your measurement first. Suggestions appear only when it matches, and you check them with live placement before moving any furniture.

Curiosities c

What your measurement says about your room

A sweep recorded at your seat holds more than a frequency response. CorrectSound reads it for:

  • Room dimensions, guessed from the pattern of bass resonances (standing waves between parallel walls, and around four walls). r shows the room from above.
  • Reverberation time per octave, and whether the room is dry, typical or lively.
  • The strongest early reflection, its delay and how much longer its path is: 30 cm usually means the desk.
  • Ringing notes, cancellations and broad dips, with the distance to the surface likely causing them.
  • The speakers: bass and treble extension, tonal balance, how well left and right match.

All of it is also written to report.txt in the session folder.

The science, briefly

What EQ can fix, and what it can't

An equalizer changes how loud each frequency is played. That fixes some problems completely and others not at all, and a good correction knows the difference.

The target: even, gently sloping, within your speaker's range

The goal is not a ruler-flat line. Measured at the seat, a speaker that sounds neutral in a room slopes gently down towards the treble, because the room absorbs more highs than lows. CorrectSound aims for −0.5 dB per octave (t to change), between the lowest and highest frequencies your speakers actually reach. Below their bass limit it only cuts resonances that boom: boosting there would just push the woofer harder for little gain.

How many filters? Ask the other mic positions

More filters always fit the measurement better, but a narrow correction that fits one spot exactly can make things worse 20 cm away. With several mic positions, CorrectSound fits the filters to all but one position, scores them on the one left out, and repeats for each. It keeps the smallest number of filters whose score is within the noise of the best: only corrections that hold everywhere you might put your head.

Try it: one filter

Each correction is a "bell" filter: it raises or lowers a band of frequencies around a centre. Q sets how narrow the band is. This is the exact formula CorrectSound uses, and the one EasyEffects runs (RBJ peaking, "APO (DR)" mode).

Try it: your room's bass resonances

Between two parallel walls L metres apart, sound resonates at 343 / 2L Hz and its multiples. Enter your room and see where its first resonances fall. CorrectSound works this backwards: from the resonances it measures, it guesses the dimensions.

Make it yours

Different setup? Give the code to your AI agent.

CorrectSound does one setup well: Linux, PipeWire, EasyEffects, a UMIK-1, two speakers. If yours is a Mac, a Windows PC with Equalizer APO, a UMIK-2, a subwoofer, CamillaDSP, or a Raspberry Pi in a cupboard, you don't have to wait for anyone. It already builds and runs its simulator on macOS and Windows; the real hardware part is one folder away.

FAQ

Questions

Is it safe for my speakers and my ears?

The test sounds play at the level the room needs, 60–80 dB, and the level step stops by itself above 95 dB. The equalizer never adds net gain: a preamp lowers everything by the largest boost, so it can't clip. Boosts are limited, and nothing is boosted below your speakers' bass limit.

What does it change on my system?

While running: it pauses EasyEffects, sets the mic's input volume to unity and may change the output volume if you ask. All of that is restored on exit, including on errors, signals and a closed terminal. When you apply, it backs up EasyEffects' settings into the session folder before installing the preset.

Left and right separately?

Yes. Each speaker gets its own measurement, target level and filters, in one split-channel equalizer. A loudness difference between them is fixed with balance, not with filters.

Why not FIR, phase correction, time alignment?

For a seated listener in a normal room, most audible, correctable problems are in the magnitude response, and parametric EQ fixes them with no latency and in a form you can read and edit. FIR is on the list of ideas; the prompts above show how to add it.

Mac? Windows? Another Linux?

It runs on any Linux distribution with PipeWire, not just Arch; EasyEffects is optional. On macOS and Windows it builds and the simulator works today, but measuring real speakers needs a platform module, one folder under src/platform/. Here is how, with a prompt for your AI agent.

Can I use another mic?

Yes: --cal-file and --source take any calibration file and PipeWire input. Automatic detection and download are UMIK-1 only for now.

Where can I ask, or show my results?

In Discussions on GitHub: questions, your room's before and after, ideas, a port you are working on. Something broken? Open an issue, and if you can, attach the session folder's report.txt (it holds no recordings).

Patents?

A brief, non-lawyer look found nothing this needs a licence for. The methods are published and decades old: exponential sweeps (Farina, 2000), regularised inversion (Kirkeby, 1998), the RBJ filter cookbook, Schroeder integration (1965), cumulative spectral decay (1970s), averaging positions, cross-validation. Audyssey's multi-position averaging patent (US 8,005,228) is listed as expired; Sonos' calibration patents cover networked speakers calibrated with a moving phone, which this isn't. Not legal advice.
